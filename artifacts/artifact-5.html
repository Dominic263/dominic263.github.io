Navigating Human Bias — A Personal Value Statement

Introduction: A statement of what I stand for when bias enters technical work, and four concrete strategies for building teams that catch what any one person would miss — drawn from how large physics collaborations guard their own results.

Description

This artifact is a personal value statement on navigating human bias in AI and research, together with four strategies for acting on it. Its central claim is that bias in technical work is rarely dramatic. It shows up quietly — in assumptions about which data is "good enough," whose analysis gets trusted without a second look, and which voices are heard first in a room full of experts. The conclusion I draw from that is deliberately unsentimental: the goal is not a team of unbiased people, because good intentions are not a control. The goal is a process with enough independent checks that a blind spot has to get past several of them to reach the result.

The four strategies follow from that premise. Structured disagreement treats peer review as a habit rather than an event and assigns someone to argue against the consensus. Visible data decisions require a written rationale for data selection and labeling, and tracked provenance, on the principle that you cannot correct a bias you cannot trace. Diversifying who gets to ask questions brings cross-disciplinary reviewers in before deployment and lowers the barrier for junior people to raise concerns. Modeling uncertainty rather than confidence means saying "I might be wrong here" out loud and revisiting key decisions on a schedule instead of only when something visibly breaks.

For the portfolio, I expanded the discussion post into a designed page and added a hand-coded diagram of layered review. Four different kinds of bias enter from the left, and each is stopped by the specific strategy built to catch it — the unquestioned consensus by structured disagreement, the insider blind spot by outside reviewers. Each layer is drawn with gaps in different places, because the argument is not that any single check is reliable. It is that the gaps do not line up.

Objective

Created for the Workshop 4 discussion on navigating human bias in AI/ML, which asked for a personal value statement and concrete strategies to fulfill it. My objective was to write something I could actually be held to. It would have been easy to produce a statement about valuing fairness and diversity that no one could disagree with and no one could act on. I wanted the opposite: a specific claim about how bias behaves, and strategies precise enough that a teammate could tell whether I was following them.

Process

  1. Starting from experience, not principle: rather than beginning with abstract values, I started from what I have actually watched happen in research collaborations — the assumptions that go unchallenged because everyone already agrees with them.
  2. Identifying the real failure mode: working out that the problem is not bad actors but good-faith teams with correlated blind spots led directly to the thesis that process, not intention, is the control.
  3. Borrowing from physics: large collaborations already solve a version of this. Independent groups cross-check results before anyone claims a discovery, and provenance is tracked for every measurement. I translated that culture into four strategies for AI work.
  4. Making each strategy testable: I wrote every strategy as two concrete practices rather than a sentiment, so each one describes a behavior somebody could observe.
  5. Designing the layered-review diagram: I hand-coded the SVG so the central argument — that checks work because their gaps sit in different places — is visible rather than only asserted.

Value Proposition

Unique Value

Most statements about AI fairness commit their author to nothing. This one commits me to specific, checkable behaviors, and it brings an angle that comes from my own background rather than from a textbook: experimental physics has spent decades building institutional habits for not trusting a single group's result, and those habits transfer directly to AI development. The artifact demonstrates that I can reason about ethics as a systems problem — designing for the failure mode rather than appealing to good intentions.

Relevance

Organizations deploying AI increasingly have to answer for how a model reached a decision and who checked it before it shipped. Teams need people who can build that review capability into normal practice instead of treating it as a compliance step at the end. This statement is a working example of that thinking, and the four strategies are directly reusable: any team can adopt devil's advocate roles, written data rationales, cross-disciplinary review, and scheduled revisits without new tooling.
