When the Data Fights Back

A field guide to the three problems every machine learning project runs into — missing data, imbalanced classes, and drift. How to recognize each one, and what to do first.

Most of what goes wrong in a machine learning project is not the model. It is the data. A model that trains cleanly and scores well can still be quietly useless, and in almost every case the cause is one of three things: information that is missing, classes that are lopsided, or a world that moved after training finished.

I worked through these three scenarios in a course roleplay, and it struck me that they are the failures nobody warns you about in a tutorial, because tutorial datasets are already clean. So I wrote this as a reference for my classmates and for anyone starting their first real project. Each section covers what the problem looks like, why the obvious fix is often wrong, and what to actually do.

1Missing Data

Scenario: a telecom company wants to predict which customers will cancel. Thousands of records have no value for monthly charges, and the satisfaction survey field is empty for a large share of customers.

The tempting fixes are to delete the incomplete rows or to fill every gap with the column average. Both are fast, and both can quietly damage the model — because they assume the gaps are accidents. Usually they are not.

The trap: the fact that a value is missing is often itself a signal. If unhappy customers are the ones who skip the satisfaction survey, then deleting those rows throws away exactly the customers you are trying to predict, and filling them with the average makes them look typical. You have not cleaned the data; you have erased the pattern.

What to do instead

  • Ask why it is missing first. A field missing at random (a form glitch) is a different problem from a field missing because of the very thing you are predicting. The second kind is the dangerous kind.
  • Add a "was missing" flag. Create a column recording that the value was absent, then impute the value. The model can learn from the absence itself instead of losing it.
  • Impute with context, not with the global average. Filling a missing monthly charge with the median for that contract type is far better than the median for everyone.
  • Delete only when the missingness is genuinely random and small. Dropping rows is a legitimate option, but it should be a decision you can defend, not a default.
  • Write down what you chose and why. Six months later, nobody remembers which gaps were filled or how, and every downstream result depends on it.

2Imbalanced Data

Scenario: a fraud detection model. In the training data, the overwhelming majority of transactions are legitimate and only a tiny fraction are fraudulent.

This is the failure that looks like success. Suppose that out of every 1,000 transactions, 2 are fraud. A model that simply predicts "not fraud" for every single transaction is correct 998 times out of 1,000. It reports 99.8% accuracy, and it catches no fraud at all.

What to do instead

  • Change the metric before you change the model. Use recall (of the fraud you missed, how much did you catch), precision (of what you flagged, how much was real), and F1 or precision-recall AUC to balance them. Accuracy is the wrong instrument here.
  • Decide which error is more expensive. A missed fraud and a false alarm do not cost the same thing, and that ratio — not a default setting — should drive where you set the decision threshold.
  • Rebalance the training data. Oversample the rare class, undersample the common one, or generate synthetic minority examples with a technique such as SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002).
  • Or reweight instead of resample. Most algorithms accept class weights, telling the model that mistakes on the rare class count for more.
  • Always look at the confusion matrix. A single score hides which kind of mistake the model is making; the matrix shows it immediately.

3Data and Concept Drift

Scenario: a housing price model that performed well at launch. A year later its predictions are consistently off, though nothing about the code has changed.

Nothing broke. The world moved. This comes in two flavors worth telling apart. Data drift means the inputs changed — the homes being listed now are larger, or in different neighborhoods, than the ones the model trained on. Concept drift is deeper: the relationship between the inputs and the answer changed. Interest rates jump, or remote work makes a home office worth more than proximity to downtown, and the meaning of "extra bedroom" is no longer what it was. The model is still faithfully applying a rule that used to be true.

What to do instead

  • Monitor the inputs, not just the outputs. Compare the distribution of recent data against the training data. Inputs usually shift before performance visibly collapses.
  • Keep measuring accuracy after launch. A model with no ongoing evaluation is a model nobody is checking. Hold out recent data specifically to test whether last year's rules still hold.
  • Schedule retraining instead of waiting for complaints. How often depends on how fast your domain moves — housing and fraud shift quickly; handwriting recognition does not.
  • Tell data drift and concept drift apart. If the inputs look the same but accuracy fell, the underlying relationship changed, and simply adding more data of the same kind will not save you.
  • Treat the model as a system with maintenance, not a finished deliverable — the long-term cost of an ML system is mostly in what happens after deployment (Sculley et al., 2015).

The common thread

These three problems look unrelated, but the same instinct solves all of them: be suspicious of a number that looks good. A clean dataset that got clean by deleting inconvenient rows, a 99.8% accuracy on a rare event, a model that has not been evaluated since launch — each one is a reassuring result that has stopped describing reality.

The habit worth building is asking what a good-looking metric might be hiding, and then going to check. That is not a data science skill so much as a research one, and it is the difference between a model that scores well and a model that works.

Using this guide: the diagnostic table near the top is the part worth keeping. When something feels off with a project, start from the symptom you can actually observe, and work back to the cause from there.
